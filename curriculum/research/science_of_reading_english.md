**Context:** a government primary curriculum. English is a **second language** (ESL/EFL) for children whose mother tongue is Urdu. Goal: children reach **grade-level reading proficiency**; teachers have science-backed methods they can actually run in a 25–30 minute government-textbook lesson.

**Author:** Rumi research • **Date:** 2026-08-01 • **Status:** Decision-grade — drives real lesson design.

**Bottom line up front:** The evidence is settled. Reading is not "caught" from exposure to books; for an ESL learner it must be **explicitly and systematically taught** — decode the code (systematic synthetic phonics + encoding/dictation), build oral English in parallel (vocabulary, sentence structure), and **drill to automaticity** (decodable texts, repeated reading, spaced retrieval). A textbook-driven "read the passage, answer the questions" lesson does almost none of this. The sections below give the consensus, the scope & sequence, the drilling mechanics, the proficiency targets, and the concrete gaps we must enrich to close.

## 1. The Science of Reading consensus

### 1.1 The five pillars (National Reading Panel, 2000)
The US National Reading Panel reviewed the experimental evidence and identified **five components** every effective reading program must teach, and found that **systematic + explicit** instruction is the reliably effective delivery method for all five:

1. **Phonemic awareness** — hearing and manipulating the individual sounds (phonemes) in spoken words. Teaching it *significantly* improves reading vs. instruction that ignores it.
2. **Phonics** — the letter–sound (grapheme–phoneme) code. **Systematic phonics is significantly more effective than little-or-no phonics.**
3. **Fluency** — accurate, automatic, appropriately-paced reading. **Guided + repeated oral reading** has a significant positive effect on word recognition, fluency, and comprehension at all ages.
4. **Vocabulary** — knowing the words' meanings.
5. **Comprehension** — constructing meaning from text.

- NICHD, *Report of the National Reading Panel*: https://www.nichd.nih.gov/publications/pubs/nrp/findings
- Five components overview: https://www.readnaturally.com/research/5-components-of-reading
- Five pillars overview: https://www.reallygreatreading.com/blog/what-are-five-pillars-reading

### 1.2 The Simple View of Reading (Gough & Tunmer, 1986)
**Reading Comprehension = Decoding × Language Comprehension.** It is a *product*, not a sum: if **either** factor is zero, reading comprehension is zero. A child who can decode perfectly but doesn't understand spoken English still can't read for meaning; a child who understands English but can't decode also can't read. **For our ESL learners both factors start low — so both must be built deliberately and in parallel.**
- https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/big-picture/understanding-the-simple-view-of-reading-and-scarboroughs-rope

### 1.3 Scarborough's Reading Rope (2001)
Expands the Simple View into teachable strands that become increasingly automatic and strategic as they weave into "skilled reading":
- **Word Recognition** (becomes *automatic*): phonological awareness → decoding → sight recognition.
- **Language Comprehension** (becomes *strategic*): background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures (syntax), verbal reasoning, literacy knowledge.
- https://www.azed.gov/scienceofreading/scarbreadingrope · https://www.lexialearning.com/blog/what-is-scarboroughs-reading-rope

### 1.4 Ehri's orthographic mapping — how words become "sight words"
Skilled readers don't memorise word shapes; they **orthographically map** words: they bond the *spelling* to the *pronunciation* to the *meaning* in memory using letter–sound connections. This is what makes a word instantly recognisable. Orthographic mapping is **enabled by phonemic awareness + grapheme–phoneme knowledge** — i.e., phonics is the engine that builds the sight vocabulary. Ehri's four overlapping phases: **pre-alphabetic → partial-alphabetic → full-alphabetic → consolidated-alphabetic**. Implication: teaching "sight words" as whole-shape memorisation is inefficient; even irregular high-frequency words are mapped fastest via their regular letter–sound parts.
- Ehri (2014), *Orthographic Mapping...*: https://library.ecu.edu/networkingsummit/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/257/2019/07/ehri.pdf
- Phases summary: https://www.talesfromoutsidetheclassroom.com/ehris-phases/

### 1.5 Why systematic synthetic phonics beats whole-language / balanced literacy
- **Synthetic phonics** = teach grapheme→phoneme correspondences explicitly, in a deliberate sequence, then **blend (synthesise)** the sounds into whole words; and **segment** words into sounds for spelling. Part-to-whole. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_phonics
- **Whole language / "balanced literacy"** teaches phonics incidentally or "as needed," and leans on meaning/context/picture cues to guess words. The National Reading Panel found systematic phonics beats the mixed/unsystematic control groups. The three-cueing "guess from context" habit actively works against orthographic mapping.
- The "reading wars" are, for policy purposes, **settled in favour of systematic phonics** as the floor (UK mandates synthetic phonics). A minority academic critique (Bowers) disputes the *margin* of superiority over other structured methods — but nobody credibly argues **for** unsystematic/no phonics, and that is the relevant comparison for us.
- History & evidence: https://www.lexialearning.com/blog/the-science-of-reading-vs-balanced-literacy-the-history-of-the-reading-wars · Critique for balance: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09515-y

**Decision for our LPs:** systematic synthetic phonics is the non-negotiable spine of word-reading, paired with a deliberately-built oral-English/language-comprehension track. No three-cueing / "guess from the picture."

## 2. Phonics scope & sequence — decoding AND encoding

### 2.1 The sequence and why (SATPIN and onward)
Letter-sounds are **not** taught in alphabetical order. They're ordered so children can build real words almost immediately. Every major synthetic-phonics scheme (Jolly Phonics, Read Write Inc, Letters and Sounds) opens with the same six: **s, a, t, p, i, n**. Rationale: these yield **50+ words** early, and include continuous sounds (s, n) that are easy to stretch and blend. https://www.teachstarter.com/us/blog/what-is-satpin-hints-and-tips-from-a-teacher-2/ · https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1358113

Typical unit progression:
- **Unit 1:** s a t p i n
- **Unit 2:** m d g o c k
- **Unit 3:** ck, e, u, r, h, b, f, ff, l, ll, ss
- **Unit 4:** j v w x y z zz, qu
- **Then:** consonant digraphs (sh, ch, th, ng), vowel digraphs / vowel teams (ai, ee, igh, oa, oo), then alternative spellings and split digraph (a–e, i–e…).
- https://www.getreadingright.com.au/synthetic-phonics-teaching-sequence-letters-and-sounds/

### 2.2 The decoding building blocks, in order
1. **Single letter-sounds** (SATPIN…)
2. **CVC blending** — c-a-t → "cat" (sound out, then blend)
3. **Consonant digraphs** — two letters, one sound: sh, ch, th, ck, ng
4. **Consonant blends/clusters** — two sounds kept distinct: st, bl, tr, -nd, -mp (CCVC / CVCC)
5. **Vowel teams / long vowels** — ai, ee, igh, oa, oo, and split digraph a–e
6. **Six syllable types** (for multisyllable decoding): **closed, open, silent-e (VCE), vowel team, r-controlled (ar/or/er), consonant-le**. Explicit syllable division lets a child break `fantastic`, `garden`, `table` into decodable chunks. https://pridereadingprogram.com/structured-literacy-scope-sequence/
7. **Morphology** — prefixes/suffixes (-s, -ed, -ing, un-, re-) for older grades.

### 2.3 Encoding matters as much as decoding — teach BOTH
- **Decoding** = print → sound (reading). **Encoding** = sound → print (spelling / dictation).
- They are **reciprocal**: the same grapheme–phoneme knowledge runs both ways. Research shows **explicitly linking decoding with encoding produces gains in BOTH reading and spelling.** https://pridereadingprogram.com/decoding-vs-encoding-reading-spelling/
- **Why encoding is non-optional:** spelling requires the child to *recall and produce* the letters, so it exposes gaps that reading (where the letters are already given) hides. Encoding forces the orthographic map to be complete. https://literacylearn.com/differences-between-decoding-encoding/
- **Mechanism in the lesson: dictation.** Teacher says a sound / word / short sentence built only from taught patterns; children segment and write it. Dictation, word-building with letter cards, and word sorts are the core encoding practices. https://www.doe.mass.edu/massliteracy/skilled-reading/fluent-word-reading/phonics-decoding.html

**Rule for our phonics LPs:** every phonics lesson teaches a grapheme *both directions* — read it (decode) and spell it (encode/dictate) — in the same 25–30 minutes.

## 3. Fluency + DRILLING — yes, and here's the evidence-based "how much / what kind"

**Is repetitive practice / drilling recommended? Yes — automaticity is the entire goal of the word-recognition strand, and automaticity comes only from practice.** But "drill" here means *deliberate, brief, high-success, spaced* practice, not rote copying.

### 3.1 Decodable texts (the right practice material for beginners)
- Decodable texts contain a high proportion (target **~95%+**) of words the child can sound out from patterns already taught. They build **accuracy → automaticity → prosody** through repetition of patterns. https://www.wilsonlanguage.com/news/decodable-why-they-matter/ · https://phonicbooks.com/blogs/article/how-decodable-texts-support-reading-fluency
- They are a **bridge, used during the window** when a child is mastering the code — not forever. Once decoding is secure, move to richer authentic texts. https://readinguniverse.org/article/explore-teaching-topics/word-recognition/phonics/how-to-use-decodable-texts
- **Critical for us:** Pakistani English textbooks are NOT decodable — they front-load whole sentences a beginner can't sound out, which forces guessing. We must supply/point to decodable word-and-sentence practice aligned to the week's phonics.

### 3.2 Repeated reading (the core fluency drill)
- **Repeated reading** — re-reading the same short passage 3–4 times until smooth — is the most evidence-backed fluency method (NRP: guided + repeated oral reading improves word recognition, fluency, comprehension). Best done as **guided oral reading** (teacher/peer hears it and corrects), not silent.
- Pattern that works in class: **model (teacher reads) → choral (all together) → echo (I read a line, you read it back) → partner/independent re-read → brief timed read for the child to beat their own score.**

### 3.3 High-frequency / sight-word drills — map, don't memorise
- High-frequency words *do* need to become automatic (they're ~50% of running text). But the science says **map them through their sounds** (Ehri) — decode the regular parts, flag only the irregular grapheme ("heart word" method) — rather than flash-card whole-shape memorisation. A combined **sight-word + phonics** training beats either alone for irregular-word fluency. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4435451/

### 3.4 Retrieval practice + spaced repetition (how to schedule the drilling)
This is the strongest "learning science" lever and it is under-used in textbook teaching:
- **Retrieval practice** (make the child *recall* a sound/word from memory, with quick feedback) beats re-reading/re-exposure for durable learning — shown in real primary classrooms. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12372469/
- **Spaced/distributed practice** (little and often, revisiting across days) beats massing all practice in one block — a review of 254 studies confirms it. **Retrieval + spacing combined is the most effective pairing.** https://evidencebased.education/resource/retrieval-and-spaced-practice-study-strategies-that-must-be-combined/ · https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-022-00089-1
- **Design implication:** every lesson should *open* with a 3–5 min **cumulative review** — a rapid retrieval drill of previously-taught sounds/words (not just today's). This is where "revision" lesson type earns its keep, and why our LPs need a built-in spaced-review spine, not one revision lesson at the end of a unit.

### 3.5 Timed vs untimed
- Use **untimed** while accuracy is still forming (rushing a shaky decoder cements guessing).
- Introduce **timed / self-competition** ("beat your score") once accuracy is high, as a motivation and automaticity tool — measured against grade WCPM norms (§4), **not** to push speed at the cost of accuracy or comprehension. Fluency is a *means* to comprehension, not an end. https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/what-are-the-best-fluency-learning-targets-i-think-my-school-is-overdoing-it

**Verdict on drilling:** short (5–10 min/day), high-success (>90% accuracy), spaced across days, retrieval-based, and always in service of reading connected text for meaning. That's the kind and the dose.

## 4. Grade-level proficiency benchmarks (design targets)

### 4.1 Oral Reading Fluency norms — Hasbrouck & Tindal 2017 (WCPM, end-of-year / spring, 50th percentile)
These are the widely-used US norms (compiled from DIBELS, DIBELS Next, easyCBM). They are an **aspirational ceiling for an ESL context**, but they anchor the shape of the growth curve. https://www.readingrockets.org/topics/fluency/articles/fluency-norms-chart-2017-update · Technical Report #1702: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED594994.pdf

| Grade | Spring 50th %ile WCPM (words correct/min) |
|------:|:--|
| G1 | ~60 |
| G2 | ~100 |
| G3 | ~112 |
| G4 | ~133 |
| G5 | ~146 |

Guidance: a child **10+ WCPM below the 50th percentile** on grade-level text needs a fluency-building program.

### 4.2 What a proficient reader can DO by end of each grade (decoding + comprehension milestones)
Synthesised from structured-literacy progressions (https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-101-learning-modules/course-modules/phonics/practice · https://pridereadingprogram.com/structured-literacy-scope-sequence/):
- **End G1:** all single letter-sounds + common digraphs (sh/ch/th/ck); blends/segments **CVC and CCVC/CVCC** words accurately; reads short decodable sentences; ~60 WCPM on simple text; retells a simple story orally.
- **End G2:** decodes **vowel teams, silent-e, two-syllable** words; reads simple connected passages with growing fluency (~90–100 WCPM ceiling); answers literal comprehension questions; spells taught patterns from dictation.
- **End G3:** decodes **multisyllabic words** using syllable types + common affixes; fluent enough (~110 WCPM ceiling) that attention shifts to meaning; makes simple inferences; reads to learn. *This is the pivot from "learning to read" to "reading to learn."*
- **End G4:** reads grade texts fluently (~130 WCPM ceiling); handles morphologically complex words; summarises, infers, compares within a text; vocabulary is now the main comprehension limiter.
- **End G5:** fluent, prosodic reading (~145 WCPM ceiling); comprehends longer multi-paragraph informational and narrative text; infers, evaluates, synthesises across a text.

### 4.3 The Pakistan reality this is fixing (ASER)
- By **Class 5, only ~54–56%** of children can read a **Class 2-level English** sentence — i.e. ~44% cannot. By **Grade 3, only ~13.8%** can read English sentences. This is a **decoding-and-oral-language crisis**, not a comprehension-strategy crisis: children reach G5 without ever securing the code.
- https://www.dawn.com/news/1533623 · https://bloompakistan.com/pakistans-learning-crisis-half-of-grade-5-students-struggle-with-reading-and-math/
- **Design implication:** the biggest wins are in **G1–G3 code-securing** (phonemic awareness → phonics → decodable fluency) plus **oral English**. Do NOT assume the textbook's grade level = the child's reading level; a G5 class will contain many G1–G2 decoders. LPs need built-in low-floor entry and cumulative review so a struggling decoder isn't stranded.

## 5. ESL/EFL specifics — English reading for Urdu-L1 children

The Simple View makes the ESL challenge explicit: **both** decoding **and** language comprehension start low in the target language, so both must be taught — and you cannot comprehend what you decode if you don't know the English words.

### 5.1 Positive transfer from Urdu literacy
- **Phonological awareness transfers across languages** — it's a largely language-independent, metalinguistic skill. A child who can hear/segment sounds in Urdu can apply that skill to English. The strongest predictors of English word-reading are **L1 + L2 phonological processing, L1 reading, and L2 vocabulary.** https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/0161-1461(2007/026) · https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-016-9669-1
- Urdu (Perso-Arabic, RTL, largely transparent) and English (Latin, LTR, deep/opaque orthography) are different **scripts and systems** — so print concepts and the *specific* letter–sound mappings don't transfer, but the **underlying phonemic-awareness and "print maps to sound" insight does.** https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/8/1/86
- **Leverage it:** use children's Urdu phonemic awareness as a bridge ("you can clap the sounds in [Urdu word] — now let's do it in English"), and don't re-teach the *concept* of sound-blending from zero.

### 5.2 Phoneme differences — English sounds NOT in Urdu (explicit articulation teaching)
Some English contrasts don't exist in Urdu and are predictable error points that need **explicit mouth-position / minimal-pair** teaching, not exposure:
- **/θ/ and /ð/** ("th" in *think* / *this*) — no Urdu equivalent; often replaced with /t/, /d/ or /s/.
- **/v/ vs /w/** — Urdu has one phoneme spanning both; *vet/wet* get confused.
- **/p/ aspiration**, short-vowel contrasts (/æ/ vs /ɛ/ in *cat/kept*), and final consonant clusters (*desks*) are hard.
- Assessment tools for Urdu–English bilinguals are deliberately built around Urdu's phonemic inventory rather than translated from English, precisely because of these gaps. https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1572807/full
- **Design implication:** phonics LPs must include a brief **articulation / minimal-pair drill** for the specific English phonemes absent from Urdu when those graphemes come up (th, v/w, short vowels).

### 5.3 Oral-language-first and vocabulary load
- Because Language Comprehension is a *multiplier*, an ESL reader who decodes a word they've never heard gets **nothing**. Oral English (hearing, saying, understanding words and sentence patterns) must be **built before/alongside** reading them. Vocabulary is repeatedly the top predictor and the main comprehension limiter past G3.
- **Text must be ~95–98% known words** for an ESL reader to comprehend and to pick up new words incidentally; below that, comprehension collapses and no incidental learning happens. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-4781.2011.01146.x
- **Design implication:** **pre-teach vocabulary and oral sentence patterns BEFORE the reading**, keep new-word load low per text, and front-load an oral-language component. This is exactly why `pre_reading` and `oral_communication` lesson types are load-bearing for us, not filler.

### 5.4 The role of L1 (Urdu) in the English lesson
- Judicious L1 use (Urdu) to **explain meaning, check comprehension, and activate background knowledge** is supportive, not harmful — the metacognitive/comprehension-strategy layer transfers. The English *skill practice* (decoding, blending, oral production) stays in English. Colorín Colorado / cross-linguistic transfer literature supports strategic L1 scaffolding. https://www.colorincolorado.org/article/fostering-literacy-development-english-language-learners

### 5.5 Does phonics work for ESL / in Pakistan? Yes.
- **Jolly Phonics in Pakistan:** AFAQ foundation pilot (6 schools, 108 pupils) and multiple low-cost-private-school pilots showed significant reading-score and oral-reading-fluency gains vs. traditional teaching. https://jolly2.s3.amazonaws.com/Research/Afaq%20report%202015-16.pdf · https://www.qrjsocial.com/index.php/38/article/download/687/608/1393
- Systematic synthetic phonics + Science of Reading is endorsed but under-implemented in Pakistan — the gap is delivery, not evidence. **This is precisely the enrichment opportunity for our LPs.** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2331186X.2025.2556354

## 6. DELIVERABLE A — Recommended G1–G5 English phonics/reading scope & sequence

Two-track design (Simple View): **Track A = Word Recognition (phonics/decoding/encoding/fluency)**; **Track B = Language Comprehension (oral English, vocabulary, syntax, comprehension)**. Both run every grade; the balance shifts from A-heavy (G1) to B-heavy (G4–5).

| Grade | Track A — Word recognition (decode + encode + fluency) | Track B — Oral language & comprehension |
|------:|:--|:--|
| **G1** | Phonemic awareness (rhyme, initial/final sound, blend/segment orally). Letter-sounds SATPIN → full alphabet + digraphs sh/ch/th/ck. **CVC** blend (read) + segment (spell/dictate). Decodable CVC sentences. Heart-word mapping for a few HF words. | Heavy oral English: naming vocabulary, simple SVO sentences, TPR/listen-and-do, sung/chanted patterns. Story listening + oral retell (in English, L1 to check meaning). |
| **G2** | Consonant blends (CCVC/CVCC), vowel teams (ai/ee/oa/oo/igh), silent-e (VCE), two-syllable words. Dictation of taught patterns. Repeated reading of decodable passages → ~90–100 WCPM ceiling. | Expand vocabulary (topic sets), question forms, past/present tense orally. Literal comprehension (who/what/where), oral + simple written answers. |
| **G3** | **Six syllable types** + syllable division; common affixes (-s/-ed/-ing/un-/re-). Multisyllabic decoding. Fluency: prosody + partner repeated reading, ~110 WCPM ceiling. Spelling rules (doubling, drop-e). | Sentence structure (conjunctions, clauses) orally + in reading. Inference (why/how), sequencing, main idea. Vocabulary depth (synonyms, word families). **Pivot: learning-to-read → reading-to-learn.** |
| **G4** | Morphology (roots, prefixes, suffixes), less-common vowel patterns, schwa. Fluency to ~130 WCPM on grade text; automaticity so attention is on meaning. | Academic + Tier-2 vocabulary; summarising, comparing, evidence-from-text. Longer narrative + informational text. Grammar in service of comprehension/writing. |
| **G5** | Advanced multisyllabic + morphological decoding; fluency ~145 WCPM, prosodic. Focus shifts to vocabulary/comprehension as limiter. | Synthesise across text, evaluate, infer author purpose; rich vocabulary; paragraph writing; oral discussion/presentation. |

**Cross-cutting spine (all grades):** every lesson opens with a **spaced cumulative-review retrieval drill** of prior sounds/words; every phonics lesson does **both decode and encode**; texts kept at **≥95% decodable / ≥95–98% known-word** for the child's actual level (not nominal grade).

## 7. DELIVERABLE B — What each of our 7 lesson TYPES must contain to be SoR-aligned

For each type: the **must-have components** (add these where the textbook lesson lacks them) and the **evidence anchor**.

### 1. `pre_reading`
**Must contain:** (a) **activate background knowledge** (L1 ok) so the text is comprehensible; (b) **pre-teach 3–6 key vocabulary words** orally with meaning + a picture/gesture (get text to ~95–98% known words); (c) set a purpose for reading; (d) a quick **oral sentence-pattern preview**. *Anchor: Simple View (comprehension needs language), vocabulary-coverage threshold (§5.3).*

### 2. `phonics`  — **the highest-leverage type; must be fully specified**
**Must contain, in order, every lesson:**
1. **Spaced review drill** (3–5 min retrieval of prior sounds/HF words).
2. **Explicit new grapheme–phoneme teaching** — say the sound, show the letter, model the mouth (add **articulation / minimal-pair** note if the phoneme is absent in Urdu, e.g. th, v/w).
3. **Blending (decode)** — sound out → blend taught words (I do / we do / you do).
4. **Segmenting + encoding (spell/dictate)** — teacher dictates sounds/words/one short sentence built only from taught patterns; children write. *(This is the most commonly missing piece.)*
5. **Decodable connected text** — read words/sentences ≥95% decodable from taught patterns; brief repeated read.
6. **Quick check** (retrieval): read + spell 2–3 target words.
*Anchor: NRP systematic phonics; decode+encode reciprocity (§2.3); decodable texts (§3.1); retrieval+spacing (§3.4).*

### 3. `reading_comprehension`
**Must contain:** (a) **fluency-first** — model read → choral/echo → partner **repeated read** of the passage (comprehension rides on fluency); (b) **explicit comprehension strategy** taught (predict / question / clarify / summarise / infer) with I-do/we-do/you-do; (c) mix of **literal + inferential** questions; (d) oral discussion before written answers; (e) L1 permitted for meaning-checking. *Anchor: NRP fluency + comprehension-strategy instruction; Reading Rope language strands.*

### 4. `vocabulary_grammar`
**Must contain:** (a) **explicit word-meaning teaching** — child-friendly definition + example + non-example + use it in a sentence (not "look it up"); (b) **word-family / morphology** work (prefix/suffix/root) from G3+; (c) grammar taught **orally first**, then in reading/writing, as sentence structure (syntax strand) — not decontextualised rules; (d) spaced review of prior words. *Anchor: vocabulary as top L2 comprehension predictor (§5.3); morphology in decoding (§2.2).*

### 5. `writing`  — **this is where ENCODING lives; must be more than copying**
**Must contain:** (a) **dictation** of words/sentences built from taught phonics patterns (sound→print); (b) **guided writing** with a sentence frame → independent sentence(s); (c) apply taught spelling patterns/rules; (d) for older grades, short paragraph with structure. **Copying from the board alone is NOT encoding** and must be upgraded. *Anchor: decode+encode reciprocity, dictation as core encoding practice (§2.3).*

### 6. `oral_communication`  — **load-bearing for ESL, not filler**
**Must contain:** (a) **structured oral English production** — TPR/listen-and-do, sentence-pattern drills, dialogue/role-play, describe-a-picture; (b) **pronunciation/articulation** of English-specific phonemes (th, v/w, short vowels) via minimal pairs; (c) **listening comprehension** (story listening + oral retell). This builds the Language Comprehension multiplier that reading depends on. *Anchor: Simple View, oral-language-first for ESL (§5.3).*

### 7. `revision`
**Must contain:** (a) **cumulative retrieval practice** across the unit's sounds/words/patterns (not just the last lesson) — low-stakes, with feedback; (b) **mixed/interleaved** items; (c) a **timed self-competition** fluency read (once accuracy is secure) against the grade WCPM target; (d) re-teach the items missed. *Anchor: retrieval + spaced/distributed practice is the single strongest learning-science lever (§3.4).*

## 8. DELIVERABLE C — Concrete drilling/practice mechanics for a 25–30 min lesson

A phonics lesson, minute-by-minute (adapt the same skeleton for other types):

| Min | Activity | Mechanic |
|----:|:--|:--|
| 0–4 | **Warm-up spaced review** | Flash prior graphemes; children say the sound (choral + a few individual = retrieval). Quick-write 3 known HF/pattern words on slates. |
| 4–8 | **Phonemic awareness** | Oral blend & segment: teacher says /c/-/a/-/t/, children blend "cat"; then reverse (segment "map" → /m/-/a/-/p/). No print — pure sound. Bridge from Urdu sound-clapping. |
| 8–14 | **New grapheme (I do → we do)** | Teacher shows letter, says sound, models mouth (+ minimal pair if English-only phoneme). Children air-write + say. Build 4–5 words with letter cards (blend to read). |
| 14–19 | **Encoding / dictation (you do)** | Teacher dictates 4–5 words + 1 short sentence using only taught patterns; children segment and write on slate/notebook; reveal-and-check. |
| 19–25 | **Decodable text + repeated read** | Read a ≥95%-decodable sentence set: model → choral → echo → partner re-read (2×). Struggling child re-reads with teacher. |
| 25–30 | **Retrieval exit check** | Each child reads + spells 2 target words; note who missed for tomorrow's review. |

**Dosage rules:** keep each drill **short (2–6 min)**, **>90% success**, **spaced across days**, **retrieval not re-reading**, **untimed until accurate → timed self-competition after**. Peers/partners multiply "oral reading heard per child" in a 40-pupil class.

## 9. DELIVERABLE D — Proficiency targets to design toward (per grade)

| By end of | Decoding | Fluency (WCPM ceiling, aspirational) | Comprehension | Oral English |
|:--|:--|:--|:--|:--|
| **G1** | All letter-sounds + sh/ch/th/ck; blend/spell CVC, CCVC/CVCC | ~60 on simple decodable text | Oral retell of a simple story; literal recall | Names common objects; produces simple SVO sentences |
| **G2** | Vowel teams, silent-e, 2-syllable words; dictates taught patterns | ~90–100 | Answers who/what/where on a short passage | Asks/answers simple questions; present & past orally |
| **G3** | Six syllable types + affixes; multisyllabic words | ~110 | Simple inference, sequence, main idea; **reads to learn** | Uses conjunctions/clauses; retells with detail |
| **G4** | Morphology; complex vowel patterns; schwa | ~130 | Summarise, compare, evidence-from-text | Tier-2/academic vocabulary in discussion |
| **G5** | Advanced multisyllabic + morphological decoding | ~145 | Synthesise/evaluate across longer text | Presents, discusses, argues in English |

*Set an **ESL-realistic interim band** (e.g. 70–80% of the WCPM ceiling) as "on track," but design the growth curve and the milestone descriptors toward these. Track the child's **actual** decoding level, since a G5 class spans G1–G5 readers (§4.3).*

## 10. Gaps between SoR best practice and a typical textbook-driven lesson (what we must enrich)

| # | Typical textbook lesson does… | SoR requires… | Enrichment to inject into our LPs |
|--:|:--|:--|:--|
| 1 | Introduces letters **A–Z in order**, name + one word | **SATPIN sequence**, letter-**sound** first, blend early | Re-sequence phonics; teach sounds not names; blend from lesson 1 |
| 2 | Phonics = "circle the words starting with B" | Explicit **blend (decode) + segment (encode)** to automaticity | Add blending routine + **dictation** to every phonics LP |
| 3 | **No encoding** beyond copying from board | Dictation / sound→print; spelling reveals gaps | Add a dictation block to phonics **and** writing types |
| 4 | Reading text is **not decodable** (whole sentences beginners can't sound out → guessing) | **≥95% decodable** practice text in the learning window | Supply/point to decodable word-and-sentence sets aligned to the week |
| 5 | Read a passage **once**, answer questions | **Repeated / guided oral reading** for fluency | Add model→choral→echo→partner repeated-read routine |
| 6 | "Sight words" = memorise whole shape | **Orthographic mapping** via sounds (heart-word method) | Teach HF words through their phonemes, flag only irregular part |
| 7 | Comprehension = answer literal Qs | Explicit **strategy** teaching + inferential Qs | Add I-do/we-do/you-do strategy + inferential questions |
| 8 | Vocabulary = "look up in dictionary" | Explicit meaning + example/non-example + use | Add child-friendly definition routine; pre-teach before reading |
| 9 | Assumes children **understand spoken English** | **Oral-language-first**; ESL multiplier | Elevate `pre_reading` + `oral_communication`; pre-teach vocab & patterns |
| 10 | Ignores **Urdu-specific phoneme errors** (th, v/w) | Explicit articulation / minimal pairs | Add articulation note when English-only phonemes appear |
| 11 | One **revision** lesson at unit end | **Spaced retrieval** every lesson | Add a cumulative-review retrieval warm-up to every LP |
| 12 | Same task for whole class | G5 class spans G1–G5 decoders | Low-floor entry + cumulative review so weak decoders aren't stranded |
